A website was once the obvious place to explain who you were. Then it became the place to publish, sell, recruit, schedule, invoice and talk to customers. As those jobs became more demanding, we moved them into specialized applications. The website stayed at the center of the diagram, even as the important work moved elsewhere.

A small publisher might now have a domain, a newsletter provider, membership software, a social account, an event system and a spreadsheet. Readers see six disconnected experiences. Staff maintain six versions of relationships that belong to one institution.

That is the sense in which *sites are dead*. Not that personal domains, URLs or independent publishing should disappear. Quite the opposite: they are some of the best things the web gave us. The organizing unit behind a public address should be richer than a collection of documents.

## Search is changing the route to a reader

In [Pew Research Center's 2025 observation of Google searches](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), people clicked a conventional result in 8% of visits with an AI summary, compared with 15% when no summary appeared. That observation does not prove AI caused the difference. It does show why publishers should question whether the familiar search-to-homepage journey can sustain their work alone.

A person may discover an article in a social feed, an AI answer, a friend's collection or a community discussion. The original author, sources, corrections and useful relationships ought to remain accessible wherever the discovery happens.

There are precedents for separating identity from one application view. [AT Protocol](https://github.com/bluesky-social/bsky-docs/blob/main/docs/advanced-guides/atproto.md) separates identity, data repositories and social app views; [ActivityPub](https://www.w3.org/TR/activitypub/) supports communication among independent social services. Neither supplies a full business operating system, but they help establish that a social identity need not belong to a single website template.

## An exhibition with more than one front door

Imagine a neighborhood arts association. Its main site is a gorgeous magazine and calendar. An exhibition connects artists, work, research, tickets, venues, volunteers and sponsors. The artist has a distinct presence beyond the exhibition. The venue has its own calendar. A visitor saves a collection; another reads the research as an ordinary article; staff manage private capacity and orders.

One artwork should not become five unrelated records just because it appears in five places. Its history stays attributable, while audiences see permitted views appropriate to their context. An old ticket price is not a current offer. A public source is not a private planning note. A collaborator's identity is not the organization that employs them.

This is much harder than making a visually impressive homepage. It needs source identity, time, relationships, permissions, composition and accountable changes.

## The web remains the public address

Aurekai's long-term idea is to make sophisticated publishing, business and community capabilities available to many independent institutions without requiring each to invent the underlying infrastructure. Bonfÿre supplies shared native capabilities; the organizations keep their names, work, audiences and boundaries.

That is an ambition, not a claim that every described experience is already in production. We should judge it by whether somebody can enter through an article and move naturally to a collection, project or relevant application without rebuilding their account and losing the original context.

The website becomes a cover, a window or an entrance. The institution doesn't end at the door.
