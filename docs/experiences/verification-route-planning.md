# Check an opportunity before you pay for an inspection

You have a possible purchase, shipment, property, supplier, or service opportunity. The information looks promising, but some of it is still a seller's description or an old public record. You need to decide **what is worth checking, who can check it, and how much you should spend before moving forward**.

Aurekai's verification planning helps your team work through those questions together. It separates information already supplied from things that still need confirmation, compares available ways to investigate them, and prepares a reviewable plan.

**Availability:** The native cost-planning engine is implemented and tested. A connected customer self-service interface, live inspector booking, and provider quotations are still in development. The examples below show the intended workflow and use illustrative costs; they are not available inspections or active offers.

## Start with an opportunity

Record what you want to decide and where the opportunity came from. You can bring a seller's equipment list, a property address or parcel reference, delivery paperwork, or a supplier's claim. Keep the original source attached to every important detail.

Choose the outcome you're working toward. For example, you might want to decide whether to request a quote, commission a visit, make an offer, decline an opportunity, or ask the source for better information.

The first question is **what would change your decision?** Checking everything is expensive. Checking only the details needed for a buy/pass decision is a more useful starting point.

## Decide what needs confirmation

| What you need to know | A useful first check | When a further check is needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Make, model, count, serial numbers | Ask for an itemized inventory and readable label photos | Physical count, inventory inspection or repeatable device identification |
| Equipment powers on and functions | Ask for a live demonstration or dated test record | On-site boot testing or an agreed specialist inspection |
| Seller can transfer the goods | Ask for ownership and transfer documents | Independent review where seller statements are insufficient |
| Property ownership, taxes or liens | Compare official records and relevant documents | Qualified title review before relying on a legal ownership conclusion |
| Site condition or access | Ask for current photos, drawings and a guided remote walkthrough | A site visit when physical conditions affect price or delivery |
| Supplier availability or delivery cost | Ask the supplier or logistics partner for a dated response | Confirm capacity, freight and dates before committing |

A photograph can confirm a label is visible. It cannot establish that equipment works. A public assessor record can help find a property. It cannot replace a title review. The planner keeps these different questions separate.

## Compare ways to verify

Your team can compare desk research, seller-supplied material, a remote walkthrough, a partner's conditional no-charge review, a local technician, and a licensed specialist. The available methods depend on what you need established.

The comparison should show **the expected cost and what each method can actually answer**. An advertised "free" pickup or evaluation is only a potential option until that partner confirms it applies to your quantity, location and material. Travel, minimum visit fees, turnaround, availability and required qualifications can change the calculation.

If one technician can inspect several things during one visit, those checks can share a travel charge. If different specialists are required, their fees remain separate.

### Equipment example: 35 desktops in Houston

A buyer is considering 35 HP desktops described by a seller. Exact models, configuration, operational condition and transfer authority are not yet independently confirmed.

A sensible first pass asks for an inventory, label photos, representative power-on evidence, pickup access and the seller's transfer basis. The buyer may decide these answers are enough to pass on the lot, avoiding a paid visit.

If a physical inspection becomes worthwhile, the plan groups compatible tasks: confirm count, capture serial numbers and boot a representative sample during one trip. The buyer chooses the sample size and whether a full device-by-device check is required. That choice determines the work and changes the price.

**Possible result:** Hold pending seller documents; request a priced technician visit; or decline without additional spending. There is no implied engagement with 2nd Life or an agreed sale.

### Property example: a Dallas acquisition

A property team is assessing a possible estate-related acquisition. Ownership and legal transfer questions are more consequential than a basic visual condition check.

The team starts with the parcel, its original public records and any seller-provided material. It can compare taxes, location and current condition, then decide whether the opportunity warrants a qualified title review or an on-site inspection.

A remote property tour may help evaluate condition. It will not close a title question. Title work remains a separate specialist requirement even if the rest of the visit is inexpensive.

**Possible result:** Request missing information, seek a professional review, revise the offer assumptions or stop work. No public-record match by itself establishes title, an inheritance claim, or permission to contact someone.

## Read the comparison

A useful comparison answers five practical questions:

1. **Coverage:** Which questions will this route resolve, and which will remain open?
2. **Expected expense:** What is the total estimate, including labor, travel and any fixed charges?
3. **Confidence in the price:** Is it a rough estimate, an advertised conditional service or an actual dated quote?
4. **Time and availability:** Can this provider do the work in the required location and window?
5. **Decision:** Is the likely value of resolving these questions worth the cost?

A low-cost option that cannot answer a required question should remain visibly incomplete. A quote is not a booking until the provider accepts the job.

## What happens after you choose a route?

A reviewer checks the proposed scope, confirms what must be delivered, and authorizes any outside contact or expense. If a provider is used, the agreed work should identify the location, scope, timing, price and evidence expected.

When the results return, someone reviews the actual documents, photos, test observations or specialist conclusions. The buyer can then update the acquisition brief and decide whether to offer, renegotiate, schedule logistics or decline.

Planning a check, ordering it, receiving results and approving a purchase are separate decisions. Your team remains responsible for each.
