[SECTION: Assigning and Refining]

While color images of space are incredibly gorgeous to look at, there are so many reasons behind our efforts to visualize Webb's data in color.

There are many wavelengths of light around us every day that we can’t actually see. The Webb Space Telescope sees infrared light, which is a type of light that's invisible to our eyes.

If you wore sophisticated infrared goggles and flew through space, you'd see the universe similar to how Webb does. But since our eyes can't naturally see in infrared, to see what Webb sees we need to translate the light into visible color.

Webb captures multiple images using different filters that detect specific elements or molecules. Then we combine those images to make a composite, assigning different colors to individual images based on the wavelength of light they detect.

Just like you would translate a language you don't understand into one that you do, assigning colors this way helps us process the information in a way our eyes can understand. The words are always there, but now we can understand what they're saying.

After translating the infrared data to visible color, it's time to consider the aesthetics. We use principles of photography to emphasize certain details and ensure that the image is visually engaging while we work with scientists to ensure we're maintaining the integrity of the original data.

Composition is also a big part of this process. Sometimes this just means rotating an image to draw the eye to important info. And sometimes this means cropping images to create a more balanced look.

There's no “up” in space, so our team gets to essentially decide what “up” looks like for each image we release. Composing color images from Webb's infrared data is a really unique combination of science and art. Similar to composing a symphony, we're carefully balancing each note or each color and scientific detail to create a harmonious, scientifically meaningful and visually stunning view of the universe.

[SECTION: Combining and Finalizing]

Webb has two main cameras. NIRCam, the Near-Infrared Camera, and MIRI, the Mid-Infrared Instrument. NIRCam captures shorter wavelengths of infrared light, while MIRI captures longer wavelengths.

NIRCam has 29 filters, while MIRI has 9 filters. There are a large number of filter combinations that can appear in a Webb image.

We assign colors in chromatic order, blue, green, red, from shortest to longest wavelengths for both of Webb's cameras. We then carefully review what each image contains and decide which filters to keep in the final composite image based on the data quality, science, and overall look.

We'll create images from each camera, but we'll often have the opportunity to combine images from both cameras, which can produce stunning results.
